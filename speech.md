## Slide 1 — Introduction


### What I say

Good morning.

My name is Timur, I am from Kazakhstan, and today I want to talk about two problems that may seem separate at first.

The first is environmental: our water is changing because of climate change and human activity.

The second is educational: the generation that will live with these changes often learns about them from a textbook.

I believe these two problems can actually help solve each other.

* * *

## Slide 2 — We are entering a different climate


### What I say

For years, we have talked about keeping global warming below 1.5 degrees Celsius.

But just this month, UNEP reported that exceeding 1.5 degrees is now widely considered unavoidable, likely within the next few years.

That does not mean that action no longer matters. Quite the opposite. Every fraction of a degree still matters, and so does the amount of time we remain above that threshold.

But it does mean something important:

We can no longer talk only about preventing climate change.

We also have to understand the changes that are already happening around us.

And one of the places where those changes become very real is water.

* * *

## Slide 3 — Climate change becomes local through water



### What I say

Climate change can feel abstract because we usually describe it with global averages.

But its effects become local through water.

Higher temperatures change the thermal conditions of rivers, lakes and oceans.

Changes in rainfall affect runoff. Extreme precipitation can carry sediments, nutrients and pollutants into water. Drought and lower flows can reduce dilution. Coastal systems can experience changes in salinity, temperature and oxygen conditions.

And these changes interact with pollution and other pressures that are already there.

So climate change is not only something happening to glaciers somewhere far away.

It can change the river, lake or coastline that a community interacts with every day.

* * *

## Slide 4 — The invisible water problem



### What I say

And this creates a very simple problem.

Imagine a river near a town.

People walk beside it. Children play near it. Maybe people swim there.

The water may look exactly the same as it did yesterday.

But you cannot see a two-degree change in temperature with your eyes.

You cannot see a gradual shift in salinity.

You may not notice an unusual change in turbidity until it becomes severe.

And many dangerous changes in water cannot be judged simply by looking at it.

There may be important changes in the water that the community cannot see.

And in many places, nobody is measuring them.

You cannot care about a change you cannot see.

But sensors can make the invisible visible.

* * *

## Slide 5 — Two problems


### What I say

So we have two problems.

Problem number one: climate change is altering water.

Problem number two: in many places, nobody is measuring those changes.

In 2024, UN-Water reported that more than two million water-quality measurements were used for one of its global indicators.

But the poorer half of UN Member States contributed less than three percent of those measurements.

And UN-Water estimates that by 2030, the health and livelihoods of 4.8 billion people could be at risk if water quality and monitoring are not improved.

Sometimes we know that water quality is deteriorating.

But in many places, the problem is even more fundamental:

**we simply do not have enough data to know what is happening.**

* * *

## Slide 6 — There is another gap


### What I say

But I think there is another gap.

At the same time that many communities lack environmental data, millions of students are sitting in classrooms learning about temperature, electronics, climate change and water quality.

We explain what climate change is.

We show them diagrams.

They memorize concepts for an exam.

But the river or coastline outside their school may be experiencing those same changes in real time.

So perhaps the best way to teach a child that the environment is changing is not only to tell them.

Perhaps we should let them measure it themselves.

* * *

## Slide 7 — The question



### What I say

So that led us to one question:

**What if the people collecting those measurements were children?**

What if environmental education did not end with awareness?

What if students could actually build the instruments, collect real data, understand what it means, and use it to take action?

That is the idea behind AquaNode.

* * *

## Slide 8 — AquaNode


### What I say

AquaNode is an open-source, low-cost water-monitoring buoy designed to be built by students.

Instead of giving a child a finished scientific instrument, we give them the components.

They learn how a microcontroller works.

They connect and calibrate sensors.

They work with real electronics.

Then they deploy what they built into real water and begin receiving real measurements.

A node can monitor parameters such as temperature and turbidity, and our prototypes have also explored pH and salinity.

The data can then become part of a shared monitoring network.

So the learning cycle is no longer:

read, memorize, forget.

It becomes:

**build, deploy, measure, understand, act.**

And importantly, this is not a simulation.

The environment becomes the laboratory.

* * *

## Slide 9 — From awareness to agency



### What I say

And there is evidence that this type of participation changes how young people learn.

A 2024 systematic review of one hundred studies found that community and citizen science projects produced positive educational outcomes, including gains in scientific knowledge, inquiry skills, attitudes and scientific self-efficacy.

Other research on youth citizen science found something even more interesting.

Young people were not only learning science.

Their work contributed data to real coastal management and stream restoration, while building what researchers call environmental science agency: the belief that you can understand an environmental system and take meaningful action within it.

That distinction matters.

We should not only tell young people that they will inherit the problem.

**We should give them the tools to understand it — and the ability to act.**

* * *

## Slide 10 — Where this can go


### What I say

We already have a working prototype, and we tested the learning concept by having a teenager with no previous experience assemble it under supervision.

The next step is to move from one prototype to schools.

Our first target is ten schools, more than five hundred students, and over fifty kilometers of monitored coastline.

Eventually, every school could contribute its measurements to a shared public map.

If something unusual appears, students would not simply observe it.

They could investigate it, compare it with previous measurements, discuss it with their community and, where appropriate, report the anomaly to an environmental organization.

One classroom becomes one monitoring point.

Many classrooms become a network.

* * *

## Slide 11 — Final message



### What I say

Environmental education often says:

“Here is the problem. Care about it.”

Engineering education often says:

“Here is a system. Learn how it works.”

But I think we can combine the two.

We can tell a young person:

**Here is a real problem. Build something that helps us understand it.**

Because when young people can measure environmental change themselves, climate change stops being only an abstract global problem.

Engineering stops being only a school subject.

And students stop being only the generation that will inherit these problems.

They become part of the generation that can understand them, measure them, and begin doing something about them.

Thank you.
